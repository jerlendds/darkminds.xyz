---
title: Finite attention
---

Every human has 24 hours available to them every day. Well, actually, we need sleep, so the usable waking day is closer to:

$$24 - 8 = 16 \text{ hours}$$

The “8 hours” number is a useful heuristic, but it is not a biological constant. Current adult sleep guidance is better summarized as:

$$24 - [7,9] = [15,17] \text{ waking hours}$$

For healthy adults, the mainstream consensus is that most people should sleep **at least 7 hours per night**, and the National Sleep Foundation places the usual adult range at **7–9 hours**. Older adults are often placed slightly lower, around **7–8 hours**. In practice, the population mean reported sleep duration is often below that. One large U.S. self-report study estimated mean sleep duration at about **6.87 hours**, while an actigraphy-comparison study found measured sleep around **6.0 hours** versus self-reported sleep around **6.8 hours**. In other words, people often report something near 7 hours but may physiologically sleep less.

So the more accurate baseline is:

$$24 - 7.5 \approx 16.5 \text{ waking hours}$$

with substantial individual variation.

However, not everyone needs the same amount of sleep to function effectively. The key distinction is between:

1. **Habitual short sleepers**: people who sleep less because of work, stress, insomnia, social patterns, stimulants, or poor sleep opportunity.
2. **Natural short sleepers**: rare individuals who appear to need less sleep and do not show the usual impairments of sleep deprivation.

This distinction matters. Chronic sleep restriction is not benign for most people. In a controlled laboratory study, restricting healthy adults to **6 hours or less per night for 14 days** caused cumulative cognitive-performance deficits comparable to up to two nights of total sleep deprivation. Subjective sleepiness did not track the impairment well, meaning people may adapt to feeling sleep-deprived while still performing worse.

A more realistic model is therefore:

$$24 - S = W$$

where:

- (S) = biologically sufficient sleep
- (W) = sustainable waking hours
- for most adults, (S \approx 7\text{–}9)
- for rare natural short sleepers, (S \approx 4\text{–}6)

This means most humans get something like:

$$W \approx 15\text{–}17 \text{ sustainable waking hours/day}$$

while rare genetic short sleepers may get:

$$W \approx 18\text{–}20 \text{ sustainable waking hours/day}$$

That is a large difference. Over a year, a person who truly needs 6 hours rather than 8 hours gains:

$$2 \times 365 = 730 \text{ extra waking hours/year}$$

or roughly **30 extra waking days per year**.

## Genetic short sleep

Research on familial natural short sleep has identified rare variants in several genes that appear to reduce sleep need in specific families and animal models:

| Gene               |                Example variant / finding | Proposed relevance                                                                                    |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------: | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **BHLHE41 / DEC2** |                       P384R/P385R; Y362H | First major familial natural short-sleep gene; affects sleep length and clock/homeostatic regulation. |
| **ADRB1**          |                                    A187V | β1-adrenergic receptor variant; associated with shorter sleep and altered wake-promoting circuitry.   |
| **NPSR1**          | Reported short-sleep-associated mutation | Neuropeptide S receptor; associated with shorter sleep and preserved memory consolidation in models.  |
| **GRM1**           |                             R889W, S458A | Metabotropic glutamate receptor 1 variants; reduced total sleep in mouse models.                      |

The common theme is not “less sleep through willpower.” These variants appear to alter arousal, sleep homeostasis, circadian regulation, memory resilience, or neuronal excitability. In some mouse models, the mutation does not merely shorten sleep; it also seems to reduce the usual cognitive penalty of sleep loss.

But the caveat is large: these are rare-family and model-organism findings, not a general engineering recipe for humans. A 2022 population-level analysis of reported Mendelian sleep variants argued that variants discovered in unusual families do not necessarily generalize cleanly in biobank-scale populations. Habitual sleep duration also appears highly polygenic, with many variants of very small effect.

## Practical conclusion

The default planning assumption should be:

$$24 - 8 = 16$$

or, more flexibly:

$$24 - 7.5 = 16.5$$

For most humans, trying to force (S) down to 5–6 hours is not a productivity gain; it is usually cognitive debt disguised as extra time. But for a very small subset of people with natural short-sleep biology, the equation may genuinely look more like:

$$24 - 5.5 = 18.5$$

That phenotype is currently rare, genetic, and not reliably inducible. The frontier question is whether the biology of natural short sleepers can eventually be translated into safe interventions. At present, the literature supports studying those mechanisms, not treating sleep deprivation as a solved optimization problem.
